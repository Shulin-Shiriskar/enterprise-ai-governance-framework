Enterprise AI Governance Framework

1. Purpose

This framework establishes the governance model for the responsible, secure and controlled adoption of Artificial Intelligence across Austera Transport Group (ATG).

The framework defines how AI initiatives are:

* Identified
* Classified
* Assessed
* Approved
* Architected
* Implemented
* Monitored
* Reassessed
* Retired

The objective is to enable AI innovation while ensuring that cybersecurity, privacy, data, operational, regulatory and ethical risks remain within organisational risk appetite.

⸻

2. Governance Objectives

The AI Governance Framework establishes the following objectives:

1. Maintain enterprise visibility of AI systems.
2. Establish clear accountability and ownership.
3. Apply governance proportionate to AI risk.
4. Integrate AI governance into existing technology governance.
5. Protect sensitive and regulated information.
6. Establish secure-by-design AI architecture.
7. Ensure appropriate human oversight.
8. Govern third-party AI providers.
9. Establish continuous monitoring and assurance.
10. Maintain evidence supporting governance and compliance decisions.

⸻

3. Governance Principles

ATG applies the following principles to AI adoption.

Accountability

Every AI system must have an accountable business owner.

Risk-Based Governance

Governance requirements must be proportional to the potential impact of the AI system.

Security by Design

Security controls must be incorporated during architecture and design rather than added after implementation.

Privacy by Design

Personal and sensitive information must be identified and appropriately protected throughout the AI lifecycle.

Human Oversight

AI systems influencing significant decisions must provide appropriate mechanisms for human review, intervention and override.

Transparency

The organisation must understand where AI is used, its intended purpose, the information it processes and the decisions it influences.

Data Governance

AI solutions must use authorised data sources with appropriate ownership, classification, quality and lifecycle controls.

Continuous Assurance

AI systems must remain subject to monitoring and periodic reassessment throughout their operational lifecycle.

5. AI Governance Committee

The AI Governance Committee (AIGC) provides enterprise oversight of significant AI initiatives.

Responsibilities

The committee is responsible for:

* Establishing AI governance requirements
* Reviewing high-risk AI initiatives
* Reviewing significant AI risks
* Approving risk treatment strategies
* Reviewing exceptions
* Monitoring enterprise AI adoption
* Reviewing material AI incidents
* Monitoring regulatory developments
* Reviewing AI governance metrics
* Escalating unacceptable risks

Typical Membership

The committee may include:

* CIO / CTO
* CISO
* Chief Data Officer
* Enterprise Architecture
* Cybersecurity
* Risk
* Privacy
* Legal
* Data Governance
* AI/ML Engineering
* Business representatives

6. AI Ownership Model

Every AI system must have clearly defined ownership.

Business Owner

Accountable for:

* Business purpose
* Business outcomes
* Appropriate use
* Risk acceptance
* Funding
* Lifecycle ownership

Technology Owner

Responsible for:

* Technical implementation
* Availability
* Integration
* Platform management
* Technical lifecycle

Data Owner

Responsible for:

* Data classification
* Data quality
* Data access
* Data lifecycle
* Authorisation of AI data usage

Security Owner

Responsible for:

* Security requirements
* Threat assessment
* Security architecture
* Security control validation

7. AI Risk Classification

All AI use cases must undergo initial risk classification.

Tier	Classification	Governance Level
Tier 1	Minimal	Streamlined
Tier 2	Limited	Standard
Tier 3	High	Enhanced
Tier 4	Critical	Executive

Risk classification considers:

* Data sensitivity
* Privacy impact
* Decision impact
* Cybersecurity exposure
* AI autonomy
* Safety implications
* Regulatory implications
* Customer impact
* Operational impact
* Third-party dependency

⸻

8. Decision Authority

Decision authority is proportional to risk.

Risk Tier	Approval Authority
Tier 1	Business Owner
Tier 2	Business + Technology / Architecture
Tier 3	AI Governance Committee
Tier 4	Executive Risk Authority

Tier 4 AI systems may additionally require independent assurance before production deployment.

10. Governance Gates

Gate 1 — AI Use-Case Registration

The proposed AI capability is recorded in the enterprise AI inventory.

Required information includes:

* Business objective
* Business owner
* Technology owner
* AI capability
* AI provider/model
* Data requirements
* Expected users
* Decision impact

⸻

Gate 2 — Risk Classification

The use case is assigned an initial risk tier.

The classification determines subsequent governance requirements.

⸻

Gate 3 — Risk Assessment

Depending on risk classification, assessments may include:

* AI risk assessment
* Cybersecurity assessment
* Privacy impact assessment
* Data assessment
* Legal assessment
* Third-party assessment
* Operational risk assessment

⸻

Gate 4 — Architecture & Security Review

Architecture review assesses:

* Identity
* Network architecture
* Model access
* Data flows
* API security
* Encryption
* Secrets management
* RAG architecture
* Vector stores
* Logging
* Monitoring
* AI guardrails
* Human oversight
* Resilience
* Third-party integrations

High and Critical systems require formal security architecture review.

⸻

Gate 5 — Governance Decision

Possible decisions include:

Approved

All governance requirements have been satisfied.

Approved with Conditions

Implementation may proceed subject to defined controls.

Remediation Required

Identified risks must be addressed before approval.

Rejected

The proposed risk exceeds organisational risk appetite or cannot be adequately mitigated.

⸻

Gate 6 — Pre-Production Assurance

Before production deployment, assurance validates that required controls have been implemented.

Evidence may include:

* Security testing
* Model testing
* Privacy controls
* Architecture compliance
* Logging validation
* Access-control testing
* Human-oversight testing
* AI guardrail testing
* Incident-response readiness

⸻

Gate 7 — Operational Monitoring

Production AI systems must be monitored according to risk.

Monitoring may include:

* Security events
* Model behaviour
* Model performance
* Data access
* Sensitive-information exposure
* AI guardrail violations
* Prompt attacks
* Model drift
* User behaviour
* Third-party changes

⸻

Gate 8 — Periodic Reassessment

AI systems must be reassessed when significant changes occur.

Triggers include:

* Model change
* New data source
* New integration
* Material architecture change
* New user population
* Increased autonomy
* Significant incident
* Regulatory change
* Change in business purpose

11. Three Lines Model

AI governance uses a three-lines assurance model.

First Line — Business and Technology

Own and manage AI risk.

Includes:

* Business owners
* Product teams
* AI engineering
* Cloud/platform engineering
* Application teams

Responsibilities include implementing required controls and operating AI systems safely.

⸻

Second Line — Governance, Risk and Security

Establish governance requirements and provide oversight.

Includes:

* Cybersecurity
* Enterprise Architecture
* Risk
* Privacy
* Data Governance
* Legal
* Compliance

Responsibilities include:

* Defining policies
* Establishing control requirements
* Conducting risk reviews
* Providing architecture assurance
* Monitoring compliance

⸻

Third Line — Independent Assurance

Provides independent assurance over AI governance effectiveness.

Typically includes:

* Internal Audit
* External Audit
* Independent assurance providers

⸻

12. Exception Management

Where an AI system cannot satisfy a required control, an exception must be formally recorded.

The exception must identify:

* Control requirement
* Reason for exception
* Risk created
* Compensating controls
* Risk owner
* Expiry date
* Remediation plan

Exceptions must not remain open indefinitely.

High-risk exceptions require AI Governance Committee approval.

Critical exceptions require executive risk acceptance.

⸻

13. AI Incident Governance

AI-related incidents must integrate with the enterprise incident-management process.

Examples include:

* Sensitive data disclosure
* Prompt injection
* Model compromise
* Unauthorised AI access
* Harmful AI output
* Material hallucination
* AI-driven operational failure
* Third-party AI breach
* Loss of human control
* Regulatory breach

Material incidents must trigger reassessment of the affected AI system.

15. Governance Escalation

Issues must be escalated when:

* Risk exceeds organisational tolerance
* Required controls cannot be implemented
* Significant privacy concerns exist
* AI affects safety-critical operations
* AI autonomy creates unacceptable risk
* Significant security vulnerabilities exist
* Regulatory obligations are unclear
* Material AI incidents occur

Escalation follows:

Delivery Team
     |
     v
Architecture / Security / Risk
     |
     v
AI Governance Committee
     |
     v
Executive Risk Authority

16. Continuous Improvement

The governance framework must evolve as:

* AI technology changes
* New threats emerge
* Regulation develops
* Organisational risk appetite changes
* New AI capabilities are introduced
* Lessons are identified from incidents
* Industry standards mature

The framework should therefore undergo formal review at least annually and following significant AI-related incidents or regulatory changes.

Effective implementation of this governance framework enables ATG to:

* Understand where AI is operating

* Protect sensitive information
* Implement secure AI architecture
